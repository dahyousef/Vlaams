# Grading ignores the display language

What counts as a right answer never depends on the language the learner reads. A typed or
spoken Dutch answer is right for an item when it is the item's own Dutch, one of its accepted
variants, or the Dutch of a sibling — an item with the same bare Dutch, or one sharing a French
sense with it. That rule existed before English did, and switching to English leaves it as it is.

Answers are always Dutch: no exercise asks for a typed meaning. The glosses are prompts and
options. So the display language changes what the learner reads, and the grading rule decides
what the learner writes. Keeping the two apart means a learner who switches language in the
middle of a week finds the same answers accepted as the day before, and a record written in
one language means the same thing in the other.

## Considered options

Siblings by a sense shared in the language read were rejected. The four gloss-only sibling
pairs (*hallo* and *goeiedag*, *proberen* and *passen*, *vriendelijk* and *lief*, *het kaartje*
and *het ticket*) would then stop or start being accepted depending on a setting, and the same
history would have been graded differently on two devices.

## Consequences

Wrong options are a different matter: they are what the learner reads, so an option is left
out when it shares a meaning with the right answer in either language. An option the learner
would read as a second right answer is a fault in the exercise, whatever grading would say
about it.

A completeness check fails on two items that share an English sense but neither a French
sense nor their Dutch, because grading would treat them as different words while an English
reader could not tell them apart. The writer separates them in the gloss — « to know (a
person) » — rather than changing the rule.
